# ALEC — Index of State Education Freedom (2025, 2nd edition)

**Source.** American Legislative Exchange Council, *2025 ALEC Index of State Education Freedom: A
50-State Guide to Parental Empowerment*, second edition. Author: Andrew Handel, Director of the
Education and Workforce Development Task Force. Managing editors: Jonathan Williams, Lee Schalk.
Published with support from the Allegheny Foundation.
**Type.** Secondary source — advocacy scorecard ranking states on the availability of school
choice programs.
**Location.** `alec.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/ALEC_EdFreedomIndex2025_final.pdf`.

**What it contains.** A 100-point score per state across five weighted categories, reflecting
"all programs and policies that were signed into law as of June 1st, 2024":

| Category | Points |
|---|--:|
| Education Freedom Programs | 40 |
| Charter Schools | 15 |
| Homeschooling | 15 |
| Virtual Schooling | 15 |
| Open Enrollment | 15 |

**Ohio's result: rank 10 of 50, total 63.79, grade B+.** [verified] The top five are Florida
(89.87, A+), Arizona (79.62, A), Arkansas (77.12, A), Oklahoma (72.93, A) and Iowa (72.78, A);
the bottom five are Kentucky (19.57, F), Rhode Island (15.23, F), Oregon (15.02, F),
Massachusetts (14.47, F) and New York (9.96, F). Fifty states are scored; the District of
Columbia is not.

**Why it is catalogued, and the single most useful thing about it.** **The index has no spending
category at all.** Its 100 points measure whether programs exist and how accessible they are;
nothing in it scores funding level, adequacy, or distribution. That is not a defect — it is what
the document is for — but it means the index cannot be cited for any claim about whether Ohio
funds its schools well, only for where Ohio stands on program availability under the scoring of
an organization that advocates for those programs. It is the cleanest available statement of what
a choice-availability measure does and does not see, and this corpus's subject is precisely the
part it does not see.

Its point of view is stated on its own front matter: ALEC advances "limited government, free
markets, and federalism", is a membership organization of more than 2,000 state legislators, and
publishes the [model bills](alec-parental-choice-scholarship-act.md) whose adoption this index
scores. A scorecard that ranks states on uptake of its publisher's own model policies is an
advocacy instrument measuring its own program, and should be read that way.

**Underlying data are third-party and mostly also advocacy-aligned.** The index credits EdChoice,
the Home School Legal Defense Association, the Center for Education Reform, the Digital Learning
Collaborative and the Reason Foundation as the backbone of its data. None of those is inspected
here, so Ohio's 63.79 is reproducible from the published category scoring but not from primary
sources this repository holds. [open]

**Access constraints.** Freely available as a static PDF. Retrieved and read directly; not pinned
by digest and not fetched by a connector.

**Caveat — do not put this rank beside Heritage's.** Ohio ranks 10th here and 29th overall on the
[Heritage report card](heritage-education-freedom-report-card.md). The two are not in conflict
and the gap is not a finding: the editions are two years apart across Ohio's universal EdChoice
expansion, the jurisdiction counts differ (50 against 51), and Heritage scores spending and
teacher regulation while this index scores neither. Reporting the two ranks as a discrepancy
would manufacture one.
